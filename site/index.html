How to buy a watch

The essentials in three boxes. The rest of the page is detail.

What makes a good one

  • Keeps running off the wrist: a self-winding watch stops when it sits in a drawer. Three days or more means it's still running on Monday after a weekend off.
  • Keeps good time: "chronometer" or "COSC" on the listing means it passed an official accuracy test.
  • A warranty from the maker: two to five years is normal. It covers repairs if anything goes wrong inside.
  • A clasp you can adjust by hand: wrists swell in the heat and shrink in the cold. Some clasps let the bracelet out or in by a few millimetres with no tools, even while you're wearing it. Tudor's T-fit is the best known, with 8mm in five steps. The Adjusts by hand filter shows the picks that have one, and each card says how far it moves. A bracelet that only unclips without tools is a different thing: handy for swapping straps, no help with fit.
  • A folding clasp on a strap: an ordinary pin buckle bends the strap sharply at the same hole every day, and that is where straps crack. A folding clasp, like a bracelet's, is set to length once and then just opens and closes, so the strap lasts longer. The Folding-clasp strap filter shows the picks sold with one or with one as an extra.
  • A case that stays unmarked, if you're hard on watches: ordinary steel picks up scratches from desks, zips and door frames. A few makers harden the case to several times the hardness of ordinary steel. The Extra tough filter on the picks shows those.
  • A size that fits: wrists under about 7 inches (18cm) suit 36 to 40mm, and 38 to 39mm is the safe choice. From about 7 inches up, or with a broad build, 41 to 44mm usually looks in proportion. The 41mm and up filter on the picks shows those.
  • Check lug to lug too: that's the length from tip to tip, and it decides how big a watch wears more than the width does. It should be no longer than your wrist is wide across the top: about 47 to 50mm on a 6.5-inch wrist, and up to about 52mm on a 7.5-inch one. Thickness counts as well: a 41mm chronograph 14mm thick wears much bigger than a 41mm dress watch 9mm thick.

Where to buy, safest first

  1. The brand's own website, or a shop the brand lists as an "authorized dealer". Full price, but it comes with the maker's warranty.
  2. Discount sites (the "grey market"). Brand-new and often 10 to 30% cheaper, but the warranty usually comes from the shop, not the maker. Above about $500, the real warranty is usually worth the extra.
  3. Second hand. The cheapest way in. Only buy from a dealer with good reviews, and only with the original box and papers.

Buying as a gift

  • Check the return policy before you pay, and keep the receipt.
  • Ask the shop to date the warranty card. A bracelet can be shortened to fit later, usually for free.

Words you'll see

Automatic
Winds itself as you wear it. No battery.
Chronograph
A watch with a stopwatch built in, worked by the buttons on the side. Not the same as a chronometer.
Complication
Anything a watch does beyond telling the time: a date, a stopwatch, a second time zone.
Power reserve
How long it keeps running once it's off the wrist, in hours. 72 hours is three days.
Chronometer or COSC
Tested by an official Swiss lab to stay within a few seconds a day.
In-house movement
The brand built the engine itself instead of buying a common one.
Authorized dealer
A shop the brand officially supplies. Safe, full warranty.
Box and papers
The original box and warranty card. Important when buying used.
Tool-free sizing
A bracelet clasp that lengthens or shortens a little by hand, with no trip to a shop.
Folding clasp
A hinged metal clasp that opens out so the watch slips over your hand and snaps shut, in place of a pin buckle. Also called a deployant; a butterfly clasp is one that opens on both sides.
Strap width (lug width)
The gap between the lugs, the horns that hold the strap. It is the width of strap the watch takes; 20 and 22mm are the easiest to find.
Quick-release strap
A strap with a small lever so it comes off and goes on in seconds, no tools.
Hardened case (HV)
Steel or titanium treated to resist scratches. Hardness is given in Vickers (HV): ordinary watch steel is about 200, a hardened case 700 to 1,200.
mm
How wide the case is. 38 to 40mm is a normal size, and 41 to 44mm suits larger wrists.
Lug to lug
The length of the case from the tip of the top lugs to the bottom ones. If it overhangs your wrist, the watch is too big.

Every brand

First a map, then a list with the detail. Both open on the recommended brands; choose All brands to see all of them. In the list, tap a brand for its official website, or a standout watch for its page on that site. Prices are typical new ranges from general knowledge: use them to place a brand, not to budget.

Recommended is every brand marked Worth a look, plus the brand of each of the 25 picks. Each of them has a line on its row, and under the map when you choose its dot, saying why. Worth a look marks brands that give you a lot for the money and are respected by people who know watches. Fame doesn't count towards it, and famous brands that mostly sell a logo are shown faded in the list and as hollow dots on the map.

Microbrands keeps the notable small brands, most of them selling direct in small batches, whose Cred and Cool add up to 7 or more. Many are design-led, and several make hand-wound watches with the movement on show.

Desirable leaves value out: it keeps the brands whose Cred and Cool add up to 8 or more out of ten, whatever they cost, so it includes expensive watches that Recommended passes over.

Each brand has four ratings out of five: Known is how famous it is, Value is what you get for the money, Cred is respect among enthusiasts, and Cool is how distinctive it is. Unusual marks brands doing something few others do. The ratings are opinion, and model-line counts are approximate.

Cost is one figure for each brand: the middle of its typical price range for a new watch, on a scale that gives every doubling in price the same space, so the middle of $500 to $2,000 is $1,000. A brand whose range has no top, such as "$25,000+", is placed at its starting price and has a small point on its right, and brands with no list price sit apart at the far right of the cost scale.

Desirability is Cred plus Cool, out of ten. Fame doesn't count. Value is the rating from the list. Each view puts two of the three across and up, and shows the third as colour. Where cost runs across, the faint upright lines are where one of the list's price tiers ends and the next begins, and choosing a tier or a budget shades that stretch of the scale. A dot marks the middle of a brand's prices, so a brand that matches can sit outside the shading.
